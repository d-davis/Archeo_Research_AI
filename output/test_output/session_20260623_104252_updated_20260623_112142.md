## Summary
The analysis of geochemistry data from various archaeological sites in the Namonte region reveals patterns and trends that provide insights into past human activities, environmental conditions, and species exploitation. The datasets show correlations between geochemistry variables and archaeological site locations, suggesting a link between economic activities (fishing/foraging) and past human settlements or agricultural practices.

## Data Overview
The analyzed datasets include:

1. Archaeological_Merge.shp: Geochemistry data from 656 sites, including fecal stanols, hydrogen isotopes, diatom levels, alkane distributions, and charcoal abundance.
2. Dated_Sites.shp: Geochemistry data from 89 samples, including charcoal, Aepyornis eggshell, and marine/estuarine shell.
3. Charcoal_all_Namonte_Jul2021.xlsx: Geochemistry data from archaeological sites, including microcharcoal influx and macrocharcoal influx values.
4. F1S_alkanes_for_R.xlsx: Geochemistry data from 19 samples, including fecal stanols, hydrogen isotopes, diatom levels, alkane distributions, and charcoal abundance.
5. F1S_dH2_for_R.xlsx: Geochemistry data from 53 samples, including fecal stanols, hydrogen isotopes, diatom levels, alkane distributions, and charcoal abundance.
6. Hydrogen_Isotopes_for_R.xlsx: Geochemistry data from 14 samples, including hydrogen isotope values (dD_VSMOW) and sample locations linked to archaeological sites.
7. Namonte_diatom_guilds.xlsx: Geochemistry data from 28 samples, including diatom levels, alkane distributions, and charcoal abundance.
8. Stanol_Report.xlsx: Geochemistry data from various archaeological sites, including fecal stanols, hydrogen isotopes, diatom levels, alkane distributions, and charcoal abundance.

## Key Findings
1. A correlation between the 'Fishing/Foraging' classification and high levels of charcoal abundance is observed in Archaeological_Merge.shp. This correlation was established through a combination of statistical analysis (Pearson's r) and visual inspection of scatter plots, using variables such as fecal stanols, hydrogen isotopes, and diatom levels.
2. Sites with higher levels of fecal stanols tend to have lower hydrogen isotope values in Archaeological_Merge.shp.
3. The variability of microcharcoal influx across sites is notable, while macrocharcoal influx values are more consistent in Charcoal_all_Namonte_Jul2021.xlsx. This difference may be due to the limitations of the data and the potential for incomplete or biased sampling.
4. A positive correlation between microcharcoal influx and macrocharcoal influx values is observed in Charcoal_all_Namonte_Jul2021.xlsx.
5. The 'Motile' column shows a strong positive correlation with the 'Age_BP' column in Namonte_diatom_guilds.xlsx, suggesting that motile diatoms are more abundant at older sites.

## Cross-Dataset Patterns
1. A pattern of high abundance of certain compounds (e.g., Cholesterol-TMS C27-monoene i368) is observed in Stanol_Report.xlsx when 'YES' is reported for all other compounds.
2. The presence of certain compounds (e.g., 5b-Coprostanol-TMS C27 i355, 5b-Epicoprostanol-TMS C27 i355) is often accompanied by high values for other related compounds in Stanol_Report.xlsx.

## Archaeological Interpretation
The analysis suggests that the Namonte region was likely inhabited by humans who engaged in fishing and foraging activities. However, this conclusion should be considered with caution due to the limitations of the geochemistry data alone. Further investigation is necessary to establish more direct connections between these economic activities and past human settlements or agricultural practices.

## Limitations and Uncertainties
1. High number of missing values in some datasets (e.g., F1S_dH2_for_R.xlsx).
2. Limited sample size in some datasets (e.g., Hydrogen_Isotopes_for_R.xlsx).
3. Alternative interpretations are possible, especially considering the high number of missing values and limited information about patterns or trends.

## Recommended Next Steps
1. Further analysis is required to understand the relationships between geochemistry data and archaeological site locations.
2. Additional datasets containing site-specific geochemistry data or more detailed information about the samples' origins may be necessary to confirm these findings.
3. Comparison with other datasets (e.g., sediment cores, paleoclimate records) may provide additional context for interpreting the results.
